# [‘Bloom, Gloom & Doom’ Reign On in AI. Nvidia, Meta & Bill Gates. ARD #150](https://substack.com/app-link/post?publication_id=684161&post_id=212984796&utm_source=post-email-title&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=8ne7oq&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo1MjMwMjM3MjIsInBvc3RfaWQiOjIxMjk4NDc5NiwiaWF0IjoxNzg3ODYxMzMwLCJleHAiOjE3OTA0NTMzMzAsImlzcyI6InB1Yi02ODQxNjEiLCJzdWIiOiJwb3N0LXJlYWN0aW9uIn0.Nak1EK_dYbf0HhygiAN0JbjALHxpxNNMR2qwD4FFq-g)

### ...Nvidia's $96B quarter & $13B Hugging Face buy, Meta's $18B teen deal, Gates' AI alarm, & Apple's Sept 9 event.

‘Bloom, Gloom and Doom’ Reign On. Episode 150 of these daily shows, and the week has run in a straight line: Monday the world on Nvidia’s shoulders, Tuesday the need for speed, Wednesday ambitions beyond their wildest dreams. Today the harvest and invoices arrive together. Nvidia brought the bloom with a record quarter and a chess-move acquisition, Meta paid for the gloom with an $18 billion settlement for the last platform era, and Bill Gates argued the doom case in the most sobering essay of his AI writings. Gloom and doom get the headlines; the AI businesses keep blooming anyway, and that the Mr. Market ‘Wall of Worry’ tension is the whole [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/d61eb057-3d60-42a4-b5d5-1e8bf52506fa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) right now. My takes below, as discussed on the show.

## (1) Nvidia’s Q2: ‘Reign On’, Again

The quarter we set the table for on Monday: Nvidia printed $96.2 billion in revenue, up 106% from a year ago and comfortably past the roughly $92 billion expected, with data center alone at $89 billion, up 117%. Gross margins held at 75% despite the memory-price crunch, next quarter is guided to $108 billion, above estimates and with zero China data-center compute assumed. And CFO Colette Kress told the call to expect roughly 70% revenue growth in fiscal 2028 against the 45% analysts had modeled.

The stock rose about 4% after hours, snapping seven straight down days of AI Trade worries. What made the print different was how directly management took on the gloom checklist: on the ‘circular’ financing worries, the $500 billion of data-center financing guarantees and the OpenAI Ohio backstop, Kress said some will call it circular financing, “We see it differently,” with the frontier labs positioned to become some of the largest technology companies in history. On custom silicon from its own customers, Jensen’s answer: “We’re building something very different.” His line for the quarter: AI has reached its inflection point, its tokens are productive and profitable, “Now, compute is revenue.”

Then, hours after the print, the chess move: Nvidia agreed to buy Hugging Face, the home shelf of the world’s open AI models, for $12.9 billion per The Information, roughly 80 times forward revenue, after the Groq inference deal and with Stripe’s OpenRouter acquisition having just made routing across models a business of its own. The counterweight is what the WSJ calls the $1.5 trillion question: Sequoia’s David Cahn has tracked the gap between AI investment and AI revenue from a $200 billion question in 2023 to $1.5 trillion now, with real AI revenue arriving, OpenAI pacing about $27 billion annualized and Anthropic above a $46 billion run rate, but the gap still the widest number in the industry.

My take: this is a meet-and-beat reign that has now run quarters upon quarters through this wave, and the reign is not the absence of gloom and doom; it is the navigation of it. Every quarter arrives with a fresh worry wall: the AI Trade, the circular deals, the supply-demand and pricing debates, open versus closed models, China. Vendor financing at this scale is a play I have watched across multiple tech waves; there are just a lot more zeros now. The Hugging Face buy is that navigation compressed into a single deal: it keeps Nvidia the captain of the open-source ‘Avengers’ alliance we covered in July, serving open and closed models globally through its Nemotron hardware and software open and closed AI model offerings, a counterweight to the closed frontier labs designing their own chips. The $1.5 trillion question is real and it compounds; the honest answer is that the prints keep outrunning it, for now. For now the Nvidia ‘Reign’ continues, and so does the Mr. Market ‘Worry Wall’ of doom and gloom.

## (2) Meta’s $18 Billion Invoice for the Last Platform Era

Meta agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle with attorneys general from 48 states plus DC, Puerto Rico, American Samoa and the Northern Marianas over claims it designed Facebook and Instagram to addict children, ending the Oakland federal trial in its second week, with Instagram head Adam Mosseri already on the stand, Mark Zuckerberg expected next, and the four lead states seeking close to $200 billion. Meta admits no wrongdoing. The structure is the interesting part: the money runs over ten years, and only about 70 percent of it, roughly $12.7 billion, is unconditional, with the rest releasing only if YouTube and TikTok adopt comparable teen limits and pay the states themselves. Meta is, in effect, paying a premium to make its settlement the industry standard, and publicly calling on its rivals to join.

The product terms mostly run a decade: a default two-hour daily limit for under-18s, a Night Mode block from midnight to 6am, a School Mode muting notifications, usage prompts, an optional non-algorithmic feed, hidden like counts, stronger age assurance and an outside auditor. New Mexico, which already won its own case, and Florida stayed out, and thousands of school-district and individual suits continue toward October trials.

The NYT’s scale math: the penalty is about 1 percent of Meta’s market value, roughly one quarter’s profit, for a company that had already spent $2 billion on legal challenges last quarter, and its read from teenagers themselves finds them divided: some rooted against Meta outright, most expect workarounds and faked ages. The Information’s read lands on the AI point: this framework becomes the reference case for the AI chatbot and companion companies now facing the same questions about minors.

My take: the classic playbook from Mark Zuckerberg that I have watched for two decades: do what you want first, and ask permission and forgiveness later. In this case, write a check, at about a penny on the dollar of market value, and then convert the penalty into a standard the rivals must match, which is exactly what the conditional structure buys. Here is why it matters: the AI era’s versions of these choices, an explosion of AI content, AI companions for lonely teens, agents optimized for engagement, are thornier than the ones that produced this settlement, and this was the dress rehearsal. Meta is making those choices right now, at speed, with the same playbook. I would brace for more aggressive Meta moves through the AI era, and, on this precedent, for the new invoices to be drawn up again this. The bloom in Meta’s AI spending is real; so is the gloom lien against it.

## (3) Bill Gates Turns the Dial Toward Gloom

Bill Gates published what reads as the most sobering essay of his AI writings, roughly six thousand words titled ‘The turbulent AI era is here. The choices we make now are critical.’ AI, he writes, will either be “the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice,” and even in the best case he calls the transition one of the most turbulent times in human history, with no plan in place.

He organizes the risk into three buckets: jobs, because AI substitutes for human cognition itself and compresses into a decade what previous transitions spread over generations; misuse and control, from cheaper cyberattacks and bioterror capability to models that could act against our interests; and kids, where AI companions become, in his phrase, a big protected greenhouse that never pushes a child out of their comfort zone, directly adjacent to today’s Meta story.

His prescriptions are equally out of character for a software founder: new national and international institutions on the scale of the post-9/11 reorganization, some occupations deliberately set aside as ‘Human Reserved,’ and a tax on AI tokens and robots, his old robot-tax idea revived. In the Axios interview alongside, he goes further: other than robots, “the bad stuff is imminent,” with a high chance of a net negative outcome on the current course, and neither US political party, in his view, being thoughtful about it. And the same day, the WSJ ran a pointed companion piece on his old company: Microsoft is leaving investors ‘flying blind’ on its AI businesses, with Azure’s revenue undisclosed inside a blended segment and capex figures that do not reconcile between releases.

My take: Gates’ flip from positive to negative is notable, and I do not dismiss it. He is a pragmatist and one of the extraordinary builder-entrepreneurs of the PC era, the man who greenlit Satya Nadella’s first billion-dollar OpenAI investment back in 2019, and his three risk buckets are the right list. We covered his ‘some day all computers would work this way’ conviction about AI agents back in January 2024, when his optimism about the technology was the story; his essay now goes up on the bookshelf with the cautionary essays from Anthropic’s Dario Amodei and the mixed ones from Sam Altman.

But I think he may be overweighting the visible negatives against the unforeseen positives, the same asymmetry that has made pessimists sound rigorous in every wave I have watched: the negatives of a transition announce themselves early and concretely, while the positives compound quietly and arrive unannounced, in categories nobody projected. His own essay concedes the benefits case is enormous, from AI in low-income-country agriculture to medicine, and his foundation is betting its remaining $200 billion partly on that bloom. It is also part of why US sentiment on AI runs so much lower than in China and broader Asia, which tempers mainstream adoption here. Technology moves exponentially; human behavior is a lot more linear. The essay’s real value is the to-do list, not the mood. Plan for the gloom, without writing off the bloom.

My overall take: step back, and the three stories assemble into one old Wall Street picture, the wall of worry. The phrase is that bull markets climb one, and AI’s wall is now fully bricked: the circular deals, the $1.5 trillion revenue gap, the memory-cost squeeze, an $18 billion settlement for the last era’s platform harms, the data-center backlash we cover in [today’s AI-RTZ #1191](https://substack.com/redirect/984e0b52-321d-4e27-8f02-803a11376859?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and now a founding father of the industry arguing the transition itself may go net negative. Every brick is a real concern held by serious people. And last night, against all of it, the market’s most-watched company printed 106 percent growth and guided higher.

Having watched these cycles and waves since the 1980s, as my alma mater Goldman Sachs helped take tech companies public, my read stays consistent with what we have said all along on this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/d61eb057-3d60-42a4-b5d5-1e8bf52506fa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA): the bloom and the gloom and doom are both real, and they compound together. The gloom items are not noise; they are the negative items not foreseen in every tech cycle, and Meta just navigated a big one. The doom case deserves engagement, and Gates’ to-do list is a better response to it than dismissal. But the operating businesses, Nvidia and others, bloom on, in year four of this wave, and so far the market keeps climbing the worry wall.

## Gadget AI: Apple’s ‘Surprise and Shine’ September 9

Apple set its fall event for September 9, tagline ‘Surprise and shine,’ at the Steve Jobs Theater. Two firsts make this one bigger than the usual September: it is the first Apple keynote with John Ternus as CEO, the hardware chief of over twenty-five years, half of Apple’s history, who takes over from Tim Cook on September 1, the company’s first change at the top in about fifteen years. And it is expected to bring Apple’s first foldable iPhone, possibly styled the Ultra: Bloomberg’s Mark Gurman calls it the company’s most exciting new product in several years, an ultra-premium halo device in the wide passport-style form factor, with iPad-like layouts on the inner display and iPhone price hikes reportedly coming alongside. The Pro line gets its iterative year ahead of next year’s twentieth-anniversary iPhone, and the event lands two weeks after the M6 Mac mini and Mac Studio refresh we covered on Tuesday’s show, machines already in huge demand among AI developers.

My take: much rides on this one for Apple, and I like the setup more than the consensus does. The perception coming in is Apple as the AI underdog; the reality we have covered all summer is closer to the opposite. Siri AI, rebuilt on a custom Google Gemini model inside Apple’s private cloud, is the bottom-up application of AI: for the first time, the Apple notes, mail and photos of well over two billion users can be indexed and put to work locally and privately, on device and through Apple’s cloud, a whole new class of AI search and agentic services. The Apple Silicon stack runs unified from the phone to the Mac to the data center, the same inference story as Tuesday’s Mac mini beat, and the AI data those devices hold is private by design, which is becoming the scarce asset of the agent era, as today’s Meta and Gates stories both underline from the other direction. Meta, Google, even OpenAI do not have the luxury of offering that privacy posture around user data. A new CEO whose entire Apple career is hardware, unveiling a new form factor, with Siri AI deployment details due: I think Apple can run away with the AI gadget opportunity over the next two years. September 9 is set up as more bloom than gloom or doom.

### Q&A

Q1: What is MP most looking forward to on September 9?

ANSWER: Two things. First, the Siri AI deployment details: how the rebuild actually rolls out across the installed base, what runs on device versus in Apple’s private cloud, and when. That is the part that touches billions of users. Second, the iPhone Fold, or the Ultra as the top of the line may be styled: the first genuinely new iPhone form factor in years, and a test of whether Apple’s late-but-polished playbook still bends a category its rivals spent years warming up.

Q2: What is Apple’s real AI advantage?

ANSWER: Bottom-up platforms. Apple builds its position from the hardware and software up: its own silicon from phone to data center, operating systems on billions of devices, and, uniquely, the ability to put AI to work on people’s own data privately. Add the global developer base that builds on every Apple platform shift, and Apple can let others win the frontier-model race while it distributes AI to more daily users than anyone. If the agent era runs on trust and context, that is fertile ground for the bloom.

The bloom, the gloom and the doom will keep arriving together; that is what climbing a wall of worry looks like from the inside of this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/d61eb057-3d60-42a4-b5d5-1e8bf52506fa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and the businesses keep blooming through it.
