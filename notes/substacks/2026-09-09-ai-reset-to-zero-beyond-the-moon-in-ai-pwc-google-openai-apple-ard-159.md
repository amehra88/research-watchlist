# [‘Beyond the Moon’ in AI. PwC, Google, OpenAI & Apple. ARD #159](https://substack.com/app-link/post?publication_id=684161&post_id=214961469&utm_source=post-email-title&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=8ne7oq&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo1MjMwMjM3MjIsInBvc3RfaWQiOjIxNDk2MTQ2OSwiaWF0IjoxNzg4OTk1Mjg0LCJleHAiOjE3OTE1ODcyODQsImlzcyI6InB1Yi02ODQxNjEiLCJzdWIiOiJwb3N0LXJlYWN0aW9uIn0.qYZ1id1JTuvzMHc-63eWCVFYRPxFtNgZgJNWpDL6Zb8)

### ...PwC’s $31.6T by 2050. Google’s €13B in Finland. OpenAI’s Navier-Stokes Math proof, credit fight. Apple’s $1,999 Duo.

Every tech wave has a moment when the numbers stop fitting on the old map. In this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/16ad8c3c-a2ab-4bb6-b3e3-1ededf5a38b5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) that moment keeps arriving, and this week it arrived four times in one day.

A consultancy put a quarter-century price tag on AI data centers that is bigger than the GDP of the United States. Google put its biggest European bet ever in the Finnish north, on nuclear power and seawater. OpenAI said a machine solved a math problem that had stood for over a century, and the mathematicians started fighting about whose idea it was.

And Apple, on the first keynote of a new CEO, finally folded the iPhone. The theme today is ‘Beyond the Moon’, because every one of these has a number, a place, a proof or a product that sits past where the industry treated the edge a year ago. Twelve NASA astronauts got to the moon on a budget this industry now clears in a few months. We are going a lot further than that, and the reach comes with its own costs.

Three stories, an overall take, and a Gadget AI on the iPhone Duo, with the two questions I get asked most about it. My takes below, as discussed on the show.

## (1) A $31.6 Trillion Estimate for Global AI Data Centers Through 2050

First up. Global spending on AI infrastructure is now expected to reach $31.6 trillion through 2050. That is PricewaterhouseCoopers’ new Global Data Centre Outlook, reported by The Wall Street Journal this week.

The shape of the number matters as much as the size. Annual data center capital spending is running at about $800 billion right now. PwC has it at $1.8 trillion a year by 2050. And unlike every earlier infrastructure boom, which tapers after the build, this one is expected to accelerate. The reason is that the equipment inside a data center, the servers and the GPUs, gets refreshed every few years. Construction is a one-time bill. Chips are a subscription. That is why PwC says chip upgrades, not buildings, will drive most of the long-term spend. ICT equipment is about seventy percent of the bill today, and ninety-three percent by 2050. Very good news for one company in Santa Clara. (Looking at you, Nvidia).

The geography of it. The United States takes about $15 trillion of the $31.6 trillion, nearly half. Asia Pacific, led by China and India, takes over $8 trillion. Europe and the Middle East take the rest, with what PwC calls a sovereignty-driven acceleration. There is a downside scenario too, $25.5 trillion, if export controls disrupt the supply chain.

Even the downside case is twenty-five trillion. And the Journal draws the enterprise lesson plainly: for the later years of this build to get funded, AI revenue has to keep rising, which means adoption has to keep pace. If adoption stalls or pricing weakens, the back half of the curve gets hard to finance.

My take: as expected, the global investment numbers in AI data centers keep going up. These are big numbers and they keep getting bigger. A year and a half ago the big tech companies were spending two or three hundred billion dollars a year on this. They are now collectively over half a trillion and accelerating, and Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs, my alma mater, have the number through the end of this decade at six, seven, eight trillion.

Measured over years and decades, these will likely be the largest capital investments relative to GDP in history. The debates on their merits will continue. But for now, this trend is set, jagged and volatile as it may be in the near term, as markets ebb and flow over interest rates and everything else.

Put the number next to the moon, since that is today’s theme. Thirty-one trillion cumulative is bigger than the GDP of these United States, and still big against a global GDP of about $125 trillion that everyone hopes AI will add five or ten percent to over the next decade. I think those numbers may be a little aggressive, because technology, as exciting as it is in every wave, always gets diffused, and there is a disinflationary aspect to it that does not get counted.

But the professionals know what they are doing. What we are spending now already surpasses what the railroads cost in today’s dollars, and what it cost NASA to send twelve astronauts to the moon over a decade. Last December I compared [the current US AI spend to NASA’s entire budget over its lifetime](https://substack.com/redirect/e2330343-5d25-4393-9490-e79bf0239cb2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and the AI number was already ahead. PwC’s curve puts it more than twenty times past NASA’s lifetime before we are done.

I have been walking through the units of this build since the beginning of these pages. Two years ago the question was [one to nine trillion dollars](https://substack.com/redirect/d8e3668d-f115-42f8-b7f5-27bdc3a5b2dd?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and nine sounded like the wild end. Two weeks ago, in [Data Centers 101](https://substack.com/redirect/22ee7736-3c3c-4cc9-afb6-95e4e8dbff2c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), the unit had become the gigawatt campus, trillions of dollars, five to ten years. Today the unit is a quarter century.

Big infrastructure in history always looks extraordinary in hindsight. The Great Wall of China is one example, and there are plenty of others where humans spent not on things that advance prosperity but on the human psyche, defense and religion and so on. In my view these are dollars going to things with a better than fifty percent probability of enhancing societal benefits on a net basis.

Tools to do things far different, far better, far faster, at far greater scale than we have ever had, despite all the negative aspects of AI. And the part to hold onto is the refresh cycle. A road gets built once. A data center gets rebuilt from the inside every three to five years. That is what makes this the first infrastructure boom designed not to end, and what makes the financing question real, because the bill for the chips comes due whether or not the revenue showed up.

## (2) Google Picks Finland for Its Biggest European AI Build, €13 Billion

Story two. Google is spending €13 billion, about $15 billion, on AI infrastructure in Finland. It is the company’s biggest European investment ever, per Bloomberg.

The build. Three new data centers in the north of the country, at Kajaani, Muhos and Vaala, on land Google already owned, plus an expansion of its existing site at Hamina on the Gulf of Finland, where seawater does the cooling. All of it over the next two years. Sixteen thousand construction workers.

Why Finland. About ninety-six percent of the country’s electricity is carbon-free, mostly nuclear and renewables. The climate means less cooling is needed, and often the air is enough. Finnish data centers use less water and more renewable power than anywhere else in the European Union. And the heat the servers throw off gets captured for district heating. Microsoft has a project outside Helsinki that will warm about forty percent of the homes in Espoo, the country’s second-largest city.

The power deal underneath. Fortum, the Finnish utility, signed a twenty-two year agreement with Google, and from 2030 through 2049 Google takes half the output of the two-reactor Loviisa nuclear plant. There is a ninety-four megawatt battery at the Kajaani site to balance the grid. Fortum’s stock rose fourteen percent on the news, its best day in almost four years. Two more details. Alphabet raised its capital spending forecast in July to as much as $205 billion this year. And Finland already has about €43 billion of other data center projects planned or underway, from Nscale, Nebius and others, so many that the government is drafting a law requiring operators to register, just so officials can keep count.

My take: Google remains one of the best positioned among the big tech companies on these global AI data centers, specifically because of its vertical integration up and down the AI Tech Stack. Its own TPU chips go very well against Nvidia’s, and Google uses both: TPUs for most of its internal work, some now sold to external clients, and Nvidia’s chips for external customers like Apple, whose [Siri AI is humming with Google and Nvidia inside](https://substack.com/redirect/c812cd90-8b85-476a-9d64-0f6450aa47b6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), as I wrote in June. There are a lot of synergies here. I called this pattern in July, when Google and the rest were [‘backing up the truck’ on AI infrastructure](https://substack.com/redirect/eb75e240-827a-425c-9a02-ebeb3bf464b1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and the TPU advantage goes back further, to [the OpenAI win against Nvidia](https://substack.com/redirect/4c0ff5ac-0a51-41b3-9aa3-779c350aa8dc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) last summer.

That this investment is in Europe is notable, given current geopolitical trends. Google is spending hundreds of billions in the US, which should make the administration happy on the reshoring side of infrastructure. But Google is a global company with billions of daily users the world over, and it needs global infrastructure. So this is not as ‘beyond the moon’, in the sense of beyond the US, as it may seem at first glance. It is a beyond-the-current-horizons spend, and it hits the climate side, which matters to the world despite current trends and flows, and matters in Europe specifically. It is a benefit for the whole continent.

Two years ago, in the summer of 2024, I wrote [the AI power grab](https://substack.com/redirect/f0121f5f-85f6-4b0c-bef8-aa99fed32d10?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) and [‘let’s stay cool, people’](https://substack.com/redirect/280b78ab-c4cc-4040-8aa6-06743b64091e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), when power and cooling first showed up as the binding constraints. Finland is the answer to both at once: cold air, cheap carbon-free power, and a nuclear plant willing to sign for twenty-two years. And notice what did not happen. Bloomberg says the Finnish projects have not drawn the backlash that has delayed or derailed sites in the US and elsewhere in Europe. In the US, data center power bills are becoming a midterm election issue. In Finland, the data center heats your house. The politics of this build are going to be decided by whether the community gets something back, and Google just showed what that looks like.

## (3) OpenAI’s Millennium Prize Proof, and the Fight Over Credit

Story three. OpenAI says an internal model, one it describes as significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra, has solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize problem. The result is being overshadowed by a fight over whether the lab raced down a research path it learned from its own customers, per Axios.

What was solved, in plain terms. The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids move, from blood in your veins to weather on a map. Whether their three-dimensional version can blow up, develop what mathematicians call a singularity in finite time, has been open since the 1800s and is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, with a million dollars attached. OpenAI says its model produced a proof that it can.

How it was done. The effort started September 1, after OpenAI researchers heard rumors that two of the Millennium problems had been solved elsewhere, and pointed the new model at the ones still standing. On a call with reporters the company said it threw enormous compute at it, millions of dollars, with roughly ten thousand agents at work. Sam Altman said the whole thing began after hearing that Anthropic’s models had cracked a major problem, and they were curious whether theirs could do it too. OpenAI says it will not claim the prize money. It is framing the result as evidence of how fast its best systems are improving.

Now the friction. Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematician at NYU, and Levent Alpöge, a researcher at Anthropic, had been working on closely related fluid dynamics research, heavily AI-assisted, which Terence Tao wrote up on his blog days before OpenAI’s announcement. Buckmaster publicly asked whether OpenAI raced down a direction it learned from their work, and whether private material in Codex played a role.

OpenAI says it did not access their user data. It also acknowledged it cannot entirely rule out that de-identified data from their usage helped improve its models. Altman says that now that the outside work is visible, the approaches look different.

My take: the promise of AI in the sciences, from math to healthcare and far beyond, has been one of the holy grails of this technology. The OpenAI saga illustrates the positives and negatives of these tools, in how they impact, dent and change the science research process globally. There are a lot of thoughtful debates on both sides, and they need attention. It is not something that gets solved. It is something that changes the discussions around AI, on the positive side and the negative side.

And what is going on in math will also be an issue in every other basic science area, whether it is healthcare or astronomy or climate, anywhere scientists rely on new basic breakthroughs to enhance their own reputations and get credit. Nobel Prizes are awarded to specific individuals.

So the process matters as well as the results. If the AIs are accelerating through the process, just bashing through it with brute force, and the process part is lost, then what happens to how we have done basic science for centuries? Credit and professional reputational opportunities may have to evolve, given the brute force capabilities being applied to promising areas in basic science. We have seen these dynamics in earlier tech waves. They are bigger and faster this time around.

I have tracked the math thread here since the beginning. [Doing the reasoning math reliably](https://substack.com/redirect/b63f13a4-6ba1-409b-b212-faf48cbb0f4d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) was the question in the fall of 2023, when these models could not add reliably. [AI beating humans in math](https://substack.com/redirect/b901a9d1-830d-4006-b550-33c4c9ab3d0b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) was the headline in January 2024, on olympiad problems. Today it is a Millennium problem, with ten thousand agents and a compute bill in the millions. Math went first among the sciences for the same reason coding went first among the trades, and I write about that reason in tomorrow’s AI-RTZ. A proof checks. It compiles or it does not. When the work grades itself, brute force works.

The fight over credit is the part that will outlast the proof. Here is the uncomfortable shape of it. The company selling scientists their research tools can also mobilize ten thousand agents and millions of dollars to compete with those scientists on the same problem, and it cannot fully rule out that their usage helped its models get there. That is not an OpenAI problem.

It is the structure of every frontier lab that sells a tool and runs a lab. Axios has it right: the disputes over what AI companies know about your work are just beginning. Two things I would keep in view. A Millennium proof is not settled by a press call; the Clay Institute waits two years after publication before it considers a prize, precisely so the field can check the work.

And the arrival of ten thousand agents on a math problem is exactly the automated researcher that the frontier essays describe, and it showed up first in the one science where the answer can be verified. Beyond the moon, yes. But it landed on the one patch of ground where you can tell.

My overall take: three stories, and three different ways of going beyond the moon. The money: $31 trillion over a quarter century, a bill that never stops because the chips inside get replaced every few years, the technologies change, the applications change. The map: Google’s biggest bet in Europe, in the Finnish north, on nuclear power and seawater cooling, in a country that heats its homes with the exhaust. The science: a machine proof of a century-old problem, done in a week with ten thousand agents, and a very human fight over whose idea it was and who should get the credit.

These three events highlight new horizons being stretched in AI, in the infrastructure and in the application domains, both positive and negative. So it is important to track it all, and use these as harbingers of new directions ‘beyond the moon’ still to come. Each one is a boundary the industry treated as the edge a year ago. A trillion dollars was the big number. The US was where the frontier builds happened. Math was where the models embarrassed themselves. Every one of those edges moved this week, and moved by a lot.

The numbers are always going to get the drama. But the societal issues and the debates are also things that need attention, and not just the debates over basic science. The impact of AI data centers on local communities is something I have talked about a lot, and Finland just showed one way to get that right. The trillions need revenue that has not arrived yet.

The Finnish build needs a twenty-two year power contract to pencil. The proof needs two years of checking and a credit dispute settled before it counts. Going beyond the moon is the easy part to announce. Coming back is the part that gets measured. None of this changes the direction of this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/16ad8c3c-a2ab-4bb6-b3e3-1ededf5a38b5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA). It changes the scale of it, and scale is where the debates will live for the next few years, on the money, on the power, on the credit, and on the phone in your pocket, which is where we go next.

## Gadget AI: Apple Goes Beyond the Moon in Foldables with the iPhone Duo

September 9 was Christmas for a lot of us nerds. Apple announced the new iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max, new AirPods, a new Apple Watch Ultra, and, at the top of the keynote, a very nicely done handoff from Tim Cook to the new CEO. John Ternus walked on stage at his first product event and introduced the first foldable iPhone, the iPhone Duo, starting at $1,999, per The Wall Street Journal and The Verge. If you have not watched the keynote, it is worth the half hour.

The device. It was widely expected, but the surprise was that they did not call it the iPhone Ultra, as rumored, or the iPhone Fold. A 5.4 inch outer screen, a 7.6 inch inner screen, in a squat shape The Verge compares to a passport when closed. Two apps side by side on the inner screen. Touch ID rather than Face ID. IP68 rated. A folding mechanism The Verge calls smooth, with a crease the reporters could barely find with a finger, which is the thing Android foldables never quite solved. Two colors, white and dark grey.
