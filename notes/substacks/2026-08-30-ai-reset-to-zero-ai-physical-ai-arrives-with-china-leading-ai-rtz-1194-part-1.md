World LLM AI Models that can read and write around real world contexts. With real and [synthetic data](https://substack.com/redirect/586ea641-691b-4b85-82d4-045a3ff1dcc2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) powering Physical AI moves. ## 1. The Spectacle, and the Reality

Start with the fun part. Beijing’s games ran August 22 to 26. Over 600 teams. More than 2,000 robots, per the organizers, with participants from 15 other countries beyond China’s own 100+ humanoid makers, [per Wired’s Will Knight](https://substack.com/redirect/56030117-4160-42e4-90df-bfc556dc66b8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

The headline feat came from the Tiangong Ultra, built by the Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center. Reuters clocked its 100-meter dash at [8.64 seconds](https://substack.com/redirect/06fc37d6-3d46-499c-8606-7a49c23d8cb6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), averaging 42 kph. Usain Bolt’s human record is 9.58. Honor’s ‘Lightning’ robot beat Bolt too. The robots boxed, played ping pong against an Olympic champion, scored soccer goals, and cheerled in silly costumes.

Now the reality. The record-setter could not slow down. It smashed into a padded wall at the finish. It needed over fifty steps to Bolt’s 41, took nearly a second to register the starting gun, and cannot decide on its own where to run. Others blew out their motors trying to stop.

And the events that mattered most were the ones too simple for human athletes. Plugging a cable into a socket. Putting a piece of garbage in a trash can. Tweezer bean picking.

Brown University roboticist Stefanie Tellex called it Moravec’s paradox at work: things like backflips “are actually easier than plugging in a cable.” Wired also noted the caveat running through the dexterity events: humans were teleoperating many of the robots. The autonomy is not there yet. Let alone the capability to build a truly useful [humanoid robot ‘hand’.](https://substack.com/redirect/ae0a6bb7-4556-4611-bdd5-bc788c48eb24?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA)

My take: this is the same lesson I keep re-learning since Sony’s Aibo dogs in the late 1990s, and re-told on [Friday’s show](https://substack.com/redirect/cd15b8c4-72bb-4f77-8dd8-8fa40dd04f08?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) with Hugging Face’s $399 Microduck. Robot pets and robot sprinters are wonderful through the novelty phase. Then the practical question arrives: what does it DO, prosaically, daily? Watch for utility, not backflips.

## 2. China’s Position, Company by Company

Strip out the spectacle, and China’s position is formidable on the fundamentals. Yet there is a way to go to global commercial reality at scale.

We eed to break out industrial robots from humanoid robots. The latter providing the emotional, [anthropomorphizing](https://substack.com/redirect/82b132b5-16f3-475d-9706-43aa3645e053?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA)connection in racing spectacles.

The installed base on the former: about 2 million industrial robots, per the International Federation of Robotics, roughly 4.5 times Japan, the world’s number two.

The flow: China took about 54% of new global industrial-robot installations as of 2024, with Europe at 16% and the US at 6%, per the FT. Robotics is central to China’s 15th Five-Year Plan. An aging workforce gives China, Japan and South Korea a hard incentive to automate.

Thus their focus on both industrial and humanoid robots in particular.

And public sentiment toward robots, and AI broadly, runs far more favorable across Asia than in the US or Europe, about 70-80% there vs 30-40% in the US. Carnegie Mellon roboticist Chris Atkeson, watching Beijing celebrate engineering: “I am sorry it is not America.”

Now the humanoid slice, with this month’s company-level numbers. Per [Smart Analytics Global’s H1 report](https://substack.com/redirect/f4514abb-96b1-4fad-8764-e5d634d3bda1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), the world shipped about 19,100 humanoids in the first half, up 272% from a year ago. China shipped 97% of them.

And here is the detail most people miss: the IPO star Unitree of China, is number two. To the number one, also from China.

[AgiBot overtook Unitree](https://substack.com/redirect/f9197a05-f3c5-435e-bfc7-f40150b55a59?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) as the world’s top humanoid vendor, at 8,400 units and a 44% share, up 562% year over year. Unitree, the company that just pulled off the [5,500-times-oversubscribed Shanghai IPO](https://substack.com/redirect/fba2b454-5717-4783-a4f9-171357ae6cc7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) and a $51 billion first-day value (since fallen back sharply, per the FT), shipped 5,900 for a 31% share. Galbot, UBTech and Leju round out the top five. AgiBot and Unitree alone are roughly 75% of the world market.

One more China thread, fresh this week: [Chinese automakers are following Tesla’s bet](https://substack.com/redirect/10668f2a-5471-47d6-a8a6-9886440fdce2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) that robots are the next profit machine, per TechCrunch. The car-to-robot pipeline runs in both directions there: the same EV supply chain, motors, batteries, sensors, feeds the humanoid lines.

My take: China’s advantage is the manufacturing ecosystem, not any single robot. I wrote it in [China accelerates in AI Robotics](https://substack.com/redirect/e7362e9a-74dc-49e8-996f-78860799c610?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) last September, and in the [self-driving and robots piece](https://substack.com/redirect/10eb5d8c-223f-4933-b4fd-3b33ba03a0f3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) back in 2024. Hardware iteration speed, component supply chains, cost curves, a government that funds the category, and a public that wants it. That is why the Microducks of the world get built there.

## 3. Where the Physical AI Money Is Made Now

Here is the broader map the humanoid highlight reels obscure. The FT’s John Thornhill [drew it well this week](https://substack.com/redirect/5fed675b-ef77-4c51-9a3f-63bde452bfc8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA): today’s humanoids are more likely the show ponies than the workhorses of our robotic future.

The workhorses are less video-genic.

Industrial arms assembling cars. Warehouse fetchers. Surgical machines. The self-driving fleets, where the autonomy stack is furthest along and the market is real today. And drones, deploying at massive military scale after their devastating proof in Ukraine. That is where most robots work now, and where most of the near-term money is made. Some roboticists add that mimicking the human form is an odd way to build capable machines at all: thirty axes of motion where simpler designs do the job with half.

My take: this is why the category is Physical AI, not ‘humanoids’. Cars are the first big market, robots the biggest long-term one, drones the one deploying fastest. Especially in the world’s militaries. All watching Ukraine/Russia.

The players who matter most sit across all three lanes, which is exactly where Part 2 picks up.

## 4. The Timelines: Longer Than the Market’s Clock

Here is where I part ways with the current market enthusiasm in the short term. And where the sober Wall Street research is worth a closer look.

Morgan Stanley’s humanoid work, led by Adam Jonas, projects a [$5 trillion market by 2050](https://substack.com/redirect/348e697b-77ea-4ee9-bdc4-cbdc7f7d4aa2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), with nearly a billion humanoids at work.

Great headline numbers. Makes bankers smile.

However, read the timeline underneath: adoption “relatively slow until the mid-2030s, accelerating in the late 2030s and 2040s.”

Roughly 90% of those units are industrial and commercial, not rosy (sic) home Jetsons.

And the geographic split is telling: over 302 million units in China by 2050 against about 78 million in the US.

Goldman Sachs, via Jacqueline Du’s China industrial-tech team, sizes humanoids at [$38 billion by 2035](https://substack.com/redirect/90425901-d27e-4d3d-8f5f-ad140f45b57f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), on about 1.4 million units shipped that year. Their most bullish datapoint is cost: manufacturing costs dropped roughly 40% in a year, to $30,000-$150,000 per unit, mostly on Chinese supply chains, when they expected 15-20% annual declines. “AI progress surprised us the most,” the team wrote.

Hold those two anchors against the tape. Unitree at $51 billion on day one, on well under a billion dollars of revenue, and already passed in shipments by AgiBot.

Musk talking a $10 trillion Optimus, per the FT.

A $38 billion industry in 2035 does not carry today’s valuations; the 2040s do. The market is paying 2040s prices on a 2026 product that cannot yet plug in a cable by itself.

So my updated timeline view, for the record: the useful-robot decade is real, and it is mostly NOT this one.

Industrial and warehouse robots compound now. Drones deploy now. Cars scale through this decade.

Humanoids find niche factory work through the early 2030s. The general-purpose, Jetsons-class consumer robot is a decade-plus out, likely more, consistent with two-plus decades of Aibos. See my [gadget AI discussion in ARD #151 here](https://substack.com/redirect/cd15b8c4-72bb-4f77-8dd8-8fa40dd04f08?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

The research houses, read carefully, say the same. The market, these current days, does not.

## 5. The Macro Headwinds, a 2026 Reality Check

Three headwinds sit on top of the timelines, all themes we wrote about this week in [‘Fumbling the Ball’](https://substack.com/redirect/cd15b8c4-72bb-4f77-8dd8-8fa40dd04f08?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

First, the capital and cost cycle. The ‘[RAMageddon](https://substack.com/redirect/f3a8bf62-d12f-4013-b954-f21c1d0b37e4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA)’ memory-price squeeze and the [trillions being raised for AI data centers](https://substack.com/redirect/42a9a829-1e18-4c15-a4bc-b2eff8eff4a6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA)are lifting the cost of every compute-heavy device, robots included, just as big tech runs its free cash flow toward zero with trillions of debt. The disinflation machine that made gadgets cheap for decades is shifting into reverse.

Second, [politics, and geopolitics.](https://substack.com/redirect/99ff6fed-69f0-45d8-8378-e3b63247aed6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) Tariffs under consideration reach past chips to servers and devices. The FCC just blocked imports of foreign-made humanoid and quadruped robots on national-security grounds. Reshoring mandates meet the awkward fact that the robot supply chain, like the phone supply chain before it, lives in Asia. A balkanized tech world slows the very buildout the US says it is racing to win.

Third, [sentiment.](https://substack.com/redirect/801a49fd-946a-462a-b13a-d9ef3a3bf94c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) Mainstream US sentiment on AI runs negative, fanned by job-loss fears, while Asia’s runs strongly positive. Adoption follows sentiment. It is a quiet, compounding advantage for the other side of the Pacific.

## The Setup for Part 2 tomorrow

So: China builds the bodies, at ecosystem scale, led by companies most Americans have not heard of. The US still builds the best robot brains, and the prize for the standard robot operating system is enormous. The near-term money is in the unglamorous lanes. The timelines are longer than the market’s clock.

Which raises the question Part 1 leaves open: who is positioned across ALL of it? Robots, cars and drones. US and China. Chips, models, tools, and the data that trains them.

Spoiler Alert: That player generates about $10 billion a year from physical AI already, expects ten times that within a decade, and has even put the founder’s family on the mission. Monday, Part 2: Nvidia’s Long Game in Physical AI.
