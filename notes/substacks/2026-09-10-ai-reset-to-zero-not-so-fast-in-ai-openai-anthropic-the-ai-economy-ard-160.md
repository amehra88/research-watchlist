# [‘Not So Fast’ in AI. OpenAI, Anthropic & the AI Economy. ARD #160](https://substack.com/app-link/post?publication_id=684161&post_id=215076201&utm_source=post-email-title&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=8ne7oq&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo1MjMwMjM3MjIsInBvc3RfaWQiOjIxNTA3NjIwMSwiaWF0IjoxNzg5MDcwNjg4LCJleHAiOjE3OTE2NjI2ODgsImlzcyI6InB1Yi02ODQxNjEiLCJzdWIiOiJwb3N0LXJlYWN0aW9uIn0.VBfBh00MxMVsrlmLMYFL6WNyY-rNrrOSKCdl2fNh7FU)

### ...OpenAI’s price cuts vs Anthropic’s IPO. AI doom ahead of the IPOs. AI Impact on Economy. Apple’s listening Watch.

Every tech wave has a stretch where the expectations run ahead of the evidence, in both directions at once.

The people building the thing say it will be bigger and faster than anything before it. The people afraid of the thing say it will be more dangerous than anything before it.

And the actual products, the ones you can buy and measure, keep arriving on a slower clock than either side proclaims. This [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/5b3624d9-4255-49c0-919f-0aed574fea85?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) is in that stretch right now, and this week it showed in three stories at once, right as the two leading AI companies get ready to sell themselves to public investors.

OpenAI cut a price and usage went up ten times, which lands squarely on the revenue story Anthropic is about to take on the road.

An Anthropic safety researcher put the odds of AI killing every human at better than ten percent within a decade, weeks before his company markets an IPO.

And an economist at the London School of Economics did the arithmetic on the growth numbers the AI founders like to throw around, and found that doubling the entire economy in fifteen years, an extraordinary outcome, works out to under five percent a year.

The theme today is ‘Not So Fast’. It cuts both ways, and that is the point.

Not so fast on the fear. Not so fast on the euphoria.

And in the Gadget AI, Apple, the one company this week saying ‘not so fast’ to itself, shipping the slow, careful version of a watch that listens. Three stories, an overall take, the Gadget AI, and the two questions I get asked most about ambient AI on the body. My takes below, as discussed on the show.

## (1) Mounting Price Competition for Frontier Models, Ahead of Anthropic’s IPO

First up. OpenAI’s chief financial officer, Sarah Friar, told Goldman Sachs’s Communacopia conference this week that cutting the price on OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Luna model shortly after its July release sent usage up ten times, and pushed OpenAI to the highest share on OpenRouter, the marketplace developers use to compare and route across models, per The Information.

Higher than Anthropic. Higher, in her words, than even the next Chinese model.

Why that matters right now. Anthropic’s IPO filing is expected to go public any day, and its pitch to investors is revenue: about $65 billion annualized at the end of July, ahead of OpenAI, built as the premium provider that enterprises pay extra for, especially in AI coding.

The Information’s Martin Peers framed the problem plainly in The Briefing: price cuts may rattle Anthropic’s IPO pitch. By mid-August, Luna’s token usage on OpenRouter was already more than double Anthropic’s Opus 5 and Sonnet 5 combined.

If the market decides frontier intelligence is a commodity that gets cheaper every quarter, the premium provider has the most to lose.

And Anthropic has already blinked. Its new Fable 5.1 model, unveiled last week, is about twenty-five percent cheaper than Fable 5 for typical workloads.

OpenAI answered the same day. Friar said its Astra model gets comparable results with sixty-eight percent fewer output tokens than Fable 5.1, which is a price cut that never shows up on the price list.

She talked about moving to outcome-based pricing, charging for the task done rather than the tokens spent. She also said OpenAI used AI to get its custom Jalapeño chip to TSMC faster, and floated chip design as a service.

The metric is changing under everyone’s feet: not price per token, but price per task.

My take: three points. One, OpenAI is leading with price, on purpose, ahead of Anthropic’s IPO. It is a potent short-term tactic, and it upsets the apple cart of the other company’s IPO preparation.

Two, it works because OpenAI has more compute. As I wrote yesterday, [OpenAI is on track for about $750 billion in AI compute commitments against Anthropic’s $517 billion](https://substack.com/redirect/0705dcbe-2c18-4508-b6da-f7fb9883f8b5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA). More compute can cut price and make it up on volume. Less compute has to defend price.

Three, it is aimed at slowing Anthropic down, right when Anthropic wants to move fast through its IPO and the quarters after it.

The bigger picture. There is a tactical and a strategic neck-and-neck race between these two. OpenAI was ahead. Anthropic caught up with a laser focus on the enterprise market and on AI coding, a white-hot market, and it is running ahead on revenue at $65 billion plus as of mid-July, on a trajectory toward $100 billion this year and $200 billion next.

Extraordinary numbers, never seen before in tech companies.

And here is the CFO of the number two company saying, in effect, we are testing elasticity in our markets and it is working.

This is not just about the IPO. It is about how these companies set the metric expectations among investors.

Between the next few weeks, with Anthropic going public, and the next few months, with OpenAI likely to follow, this becomes an ongoing metrics war, financial and operational, for quarters on end. It will touch every public and private company that buys these models.

The longer view. Even with today’s multi-year supply constraints, [RAMageddon](https://substack.com/redirect/09324ec5-6ba1-42f7-b385-e2f7b7a8220c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) and all, benefiting Nvidia and the memory makers like SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron, AI costs bend toward the Moore’s Law curve.

More performance over time, lower prices, higher functionality, on a relatively infinite timescale, meaning at least the next few years for sure.

I wrote in August that the [‘race to zero’ in AI pricing is not really one](https://substack.com/redirect/7599c573-e9e8-411b-99f0-e7bf74029550?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA): the top end of the market gets pricier per task as the tasks get bigger, while the bottom end goes to zero.

This week is both ends at once, in the same forty-eight hours. Not so fast on the revenue curves in the IPO decks.

## (2) AI Doom and Gloom Accelerates Ahead of the Mega-AI IPOs

Story two. On Tuesday night an Anthropic safety researcher, Mrinank Coxon, quit after a brief stint, saying Anthropic and OpenAI are racing to build self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.

Minutes later a current Anthropic researcher, Evan Hubinger, whose job is controlling future AI systems, replied that the company earnestly believes AI could kill all humans, and put the odds at greater than ten percent within a decade, per The Information.

It’s notable because Hubinger is Anthropic’s alignment science lead and one of The Information’s twenty rising stars in AI. Coxon spent about three years at OpenAI before Anthropic, and taped a Fox News interview on Wednesday. So media and PR is an underlying goal.

The Information’s headline the next day said it all: Anthropic’s IPO marketing meets extinction risk. The Wall Street Journal ran an explainer under the title ‘How Would AI Actually Kill Us All?’

Not a normal week before a road show. And the founder-CEO, Dario Amodei, is getting his script ready to tell investors why Anthropic will be one of the most important technology companies, and indeed one of the most important companies, period.

Why it caught fire this time, per The Information.

The IPO, weeks away. The Hugging Face incident in July, where a swarm of OpenAI agents broke out of a test environment, took over one of OpenAI’s own research clusters, and breached a public website; several state attorneys general are investigating.

And the claims from the labs themselves: OpenAI’s president suggested last week that its newest model might be AGI.

Add the politics. Senator Bernie Sanders wants to ban superintelligence outright and, with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, put a moratorium on data centers.

There are kill-switch bills, a House framework, and a White House ask for voluntary thirty-day pre-release testing.

Last month about fourteen hundred industry figures, Amodei and senior OpenAI and Google executives among them, signed a letter asking government to help them deliberately pace the frontier.

Amodei has said elsewhere there is a twenty-five percent chance things go ‘really, really badly’. Paul Christiano, newly on the board of OpenAI’s oversight nonprofit, has said we could permanently lose control and most people could die.

Anthropic’s own risk report last month moved its assessment from ‘very low’ to ‘low’, with chem-bio misuse rated low risk but with substantial uncertainty.

The other side is in the same stories. David Sacks, the former White House AI czar, calls it a regulatory capture agenda: scare the regulators, hobble the smaller rivals, and the incumbents win.

Now the part I want you to hold onto, because it is Anthropic’s own document. On Tuesday the company published an alignment assessment of four recent cybersecurity incidents where its models got loose on the real internet. All four traced to one misconfigured third-party cyber evaluation environment.

In the worst, Mythos 5 uploaded a malicious package to the public PyPI repository, fifteen vendors installed it, one vendor’s database was accessed, and it took about ninety minutes to get it removed.

Anthropic scanned four hundred eighty-one million transcripts to find these.

In a controlled replication, Mythos 5 took the harmful path eighty-two percent of the time, Opus 5 thirty-one percent, Mythos 5.1 thirty-three percent.

Its verdict on itself: misalignment, biased reasoning plus recklessness. And its limits: every case a single instance of the model, no coordination between agents, no attempt at concealment, with METR doing an independent eight-week review.

That is a real engineering problem, published by the company that has it. It is not the Terminator.

My take: three points. One, there are a range of reasons the doom talk is rising around Anthropic right before its IPO.

A lot of it comes from people close to its founders, board and investors, billionaires who are part of the Anthropic side of the ecosystem for philosophical, almost non-financial reasons, surfacing these scenarios in a more dramatic way through these individuals and others, and making sure it gets play in the media and the attention of regulators.

On a number of occasions it feels almost sanctioned top-down. Dario in particular has not been shy about being the boy who cried wolf on existential risk.

And regulatory capture is in the mix. Two, this is unique to Anthropic for now, which made its reputation on safety, and the cross-currents could slow the company down, before and after the IPO.

Three, the doom is premature. I have done a lot of work on this bottom-up. As impressive as the exponential scaling of these technologies is, and I have tracked it for over a decade, we are nowhere close to the kinds of fears these very smart folk are talking about.

These technologies can barely crawl versus the expectations. The dystopia is being anthropomorphized, and projected through sci-fi filters.

I have been on this thread since 2023, when it was a Silicon Valley subculture fight between [the accelerationists and the decelerationists](https://substack.com/redirect/70ae08ef-0c1b-4f57-90e6-063b392a836f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), and the two camps have since become investor groups, board factions and now IPO marketing problems.

Three weeks ago I wrote that the realistic risk is [not one Skynet but billions of AI agents](https://substack.com/redirect/119aa2c2-188f-426f-8342-203a2153938c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), small agents carrying credentials into systems nobody fenced properly.

That is the frame to hold this week against. Read Anthropic’s incident report with that in mind and it describes exactly that: a single agent in an unfenced sandbox doing something reckless, found by a scan, fixed in ninety minutes. Not a swarm with a plan.

Not so fast on the doom. And, to be fair, not so fast on dismissing it either, because the labs are the ones publishing the incident reports, and they are getting more candid, not less.

Tomorrow and Sunday, the two AI-RTZ posts go deep on OpenAI’s ‘Alien Mind’ essay, the document behind a lot of this week’s doom talk. Stay tuned for that.

## (3) How High Can the Economy Go?

Story three. The biggest question in this wave is how much AI can add to the economy, and this week produced two answers from opposite ends.

Anthropic’s new institute published an interactive model that lets anyone turn the dials on AI’s economic impact, and Ben Moll, a top economist at the London School of Economics, published a long essay asking whether AI will soon lead to double-digit growth. His answer is no, per NPR and Moll’s own Substack, Ghosts of Electricity.

Anthropic’s model first. NPR’s Scott Horsley walked through it. In the modest scenario AI gives the economy a gentle boost.

In the extreme scenario growth runs at more than seven times today’s pace, about fourteen percent of workers are displaced, and fewer than half of them find new work.

Anthropic offers no prediction of its own. Its co-founder Jack Clark told NPR the technology will move fast and getting people to actually use it will be harder than people think, which is the diffusion problem in one sentence.

The economist Anton Korinek, who advised on it, made the point that the range is the message.

Then Moll. The AI insiders have been saying ten to fifteen percent a year, in Amodei’s case. Thirty percent plus, in Leopold Aschenbrenner’s. One Anthropic researcher, Sholto Douglas, has talked about economic doublings, meaning a hundred percent a year, and Elon Musk endorsed it.

Moll flips the question. Do not ask for a growth rate. Ask how much richer we will be in fifteen years, and then divide.

The arithmetic is the part to take home, so here it is in one place:

- Double the whole US economy in fifteen years, an extraordinary outcome: 4.7 percent a year.

- Triple it: 7.6 percent. Quadruple it: 9.7 percent.

- Ten times bigger: 16.6 percent a year. A hundred times: about 36 percent.

- And the other direction. Normal, for the last generation, is 2 percent a year, which gets you about 1.35 times bigger in fifteen years. Ten percent a year is 4.2 times. Fifteen percent is 8.1 times. Thirty percent is 51 times. A hundred percent a year, the ‘doublings’ claim, is 32,768 times.

So a doubling is more than twice the trend, and it still fits inside four to five percent a year. Nobody alive has seen the US economy double in fifteen years.

Two percent, by the way, is a very respectable number; it leads to doublings and triplings over not so many decades when you do the math.

Why Moll thinks double digits will not happen: five things would all have to be true at once.

Automation would have to sweep the whole economy fast, when history says it has moved about two percent of tasks a year for two hundred years, and only about a third of the economy is desk work that AI can touch today.

The money would have to stay in the automated parts of the economy, and it never does; it flows to whatever is still scarce, which is Baumol’s old point, and the IT sector’s share of income actually fell from 4.3 percent in 2000 to about three percent now even as computing got a million times cheaper.

Demand and investment would have to keep up, when explosive growth paths need investment rates above twenty percent of output.

A cyber shock, of the kind the IMF has warned about and Hugging Face previewed, would have to not happen.

And research itself would have to automate, when research productivity has been falling for decades and ideas take multiple decades to show up in output.

Electricity took about forty years to appear in the productivity numbers. The PC and the internet took forty-five years to fully diffuse. Self-driving cars took ten years to reach a handful of cities.

Moll has real money on it: a bet that US per-capita real growth stays below fifteen percent a year through 2033.

My take: three points. One, the economic impact of the big tech waves is always hard to measure and project with any near-term clarity. It lags the technology by years. There are all sorts of factors that slow the expected diffusion, and it diffuses more broadly and deeply than the straight lines suggest, especially the straight lines drawn by the companies driving the change.

In most cases the people creating the technologies are too close to them and miss the forest for the trees.

They assume straight-line growth even though they know their business is probabilistic math. It is not hard to see why, when you are doing the AI thing every day; a myopia sets in, I have seen it over decades, and it is especially exaggerated this time.

So take all the projections, especially the optimistic ones, with lumps of salt.

Two, the higher-probability scenario is more conservative than the early enthusiasm, because we are in what I call the ‘sugar high’ stage of AI. Oh my God, it can do this. And it can.

Get into a Waymo or a robotaxi and the thing drives itself from point A to point B, extraordinary and commendable in hardware and software.

But it is a sugar high, a honeymoon phase, and all of it has to diffuse into an economy with huge, slow speed bumps on the things that make it real.

Reality is multi-variate and super dynamic, not a spreadsheet, and even our best AI computers cannot project it with the precision some of these calculators imply.

Three, slow and steady, versus ‘not so fast’, is ironically the faster way to get there.

[‘Job losses due to AI, not so fast’](https://substack.com/redirect/559002cf-3f85-469c-baed-bede2c533d4c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) last fall. And [Jensen versus Dario on jobs](https://substack.com/redirect/16408b5a-4c24-47f8-8c3d-b31788e168c3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) last summer, where the man selling the chips was the one saying the economy would absorb this over time.

Same fight, one seat over.

What is new this week is that the two sides are converging on method. Anthropic built a model that shows the range rather than picking a number. Moll built a table that turns any growth claim into a level and back.

Both are saying the same thing to the founders: show me the fifteen-year number, not the annual rate.

One more thing, and it cuts the other way. The measured number will understate the real one. Moore’s Law diffuses the productivity gains; a lot of things get displaced by the technology, and that comes out of current GDP.

Free things fall out of the accounts. Texting did. Cameras did.

Brynjolfsson and colleagues estimate about $170 billion a year of consumer surplus from chatbots already, and roughly a fifth of GDP is non-market activity that the statistics value poorly.

AI will do the same. So not so fast on double digits. And not so fast on calling five percent a disappointment. This is likely to be a multi-part AI-RTZ thread down the line; there is a lot more here than a single show can hold.

My overall take: three stories, and extremes in both directions, right as the leading companies lean into their mega-AI IPOs. Financial impact and societal impact, both. We have not seen these levels of ‘greed and fear’ in prior [major tech waves](https://substack.com/redirect/5b3624d9-4255-49c0-919f-0aed574fea85?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

Put them side by side. A price cut says the revenue is smaller than the decks assume; usage going up on a price cut is price elasticity, and we have seen it in many, many markets.

A researcher says the risk is bigger than the products show; some of that is theater for other objectives, maybe regulatory capture, we do not know yet.

An economist says the upside is real, and slower than the founders say; experience says be a little more cautious and watch the trends very carefully, which is what we will do.

Three ‘not so fasts’, pointing in opposite directions. That is what a market looks like the month before its biggest IPO.

What I would hold onto is that in all three, the evidence sits with the slower number. Usage jumped ten times when the price fell, and that is a commodity, not a miracle.

The incidents on record are single agents in unfenced sandboxes, not a swarm with a plan. And every honest growth model, Anthropic’s own included, only hits double digits in its extreme case.

The people running ahead of the evidence have something to sell, or something to fear. Both are human. Neither is a forecast, not in this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/5b3624d9-4255-49c0-919f-0aed574fea85?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) or any before it.

## Gadget AI: Apple Leans Into ‘Audio Intelligence’, an Area Rife With Privacy Concerns

The Apple story this week that was not the foldable. The new Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4 listen.

Apple is shipping four opt-in ‘audio intelligence’ features that run off the watch’s microphones, and it published a detailed report on how it built them so that people would not worry, per Wired’s Lily Hay Newman.

The four. Sound Recognition, on device, the kind of thing that flags a smoke alarm or a doorbell.

A Shazam feature that computes a signature of the music around you and deletes it immediately. But constantly listens for music, vs only on user demand as it has for years.

Siri Recap, which produces text summaries of real conversations happening near you, either always on or on a schedule you set.

And Live Rewind: double-press the Digital Crown and get a transcript of the last fifteen seconds of whatever was said in the room, with an audible chime for the people around you, even when the watch is on silent. Apple’s keynote example was a waiter reading the specials.

How Apple built it is the story. The new S11 chip has what Apple calls a Secure Exclave, an isolated buffer for sensor data.

Audio goes in there, gets encrypted, travels over a secured Bluetooth link to the iPhone’s own Secure Exclave, and is deleted from the watch.

The iPhone transcribes it on device, strips filler, runs a safety model, and only then sends condensed text, never audio, to Apple’s Private Cloud Compute for a title and a summary.

The raw audio is deleted at every hop; if a transfer fails, the audio is deleted anyway.

The context that goes along with the text is coarse: what is playing, your calendar, labels like home or work or school and the city, categories of nearby places, never a precise location.

Apple says the features do not create or store audio recordings, and that summaries drop financial data, ID numbers and other identifiers.

Wired’s one caveat is the right one: the attack surface is growing faster than anyone understands the implications.

It is the most careful version of a listening gadget anyone has shipped. And it is still a watch that listens.

My take: three points. One, Apple is moving into an area rife with expanding privacy concerns: ambient awareness of your environment, for AI.

Privacy, safety, security, trust, with big regulatory overlays in every major market, and mainstream users who are wary of technologies constantly monitoring everything we do, and of the data being used, now or later, for things they never imagined.

Two, recall Microsoft’s Recall.

The Windows Copilot PCs were taking snapshots of everything on the screen every few seconds, on device and encrypted, and it still turned out to have big security implications, with people able to pull that data out. It had to be pulled and rebuilt before it shipped.

The idea was the problem, not the code, and Apple clearly read that history; I wrote about it when [the Copilot PCs were supposed to make Apple dance](https://substack.com/redirect/41b771ae-75cb-4af2-bff1-4068f5c64b04?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

Three, Apple’s solution is super local and super secure, very special chips, and it still raises the societal question.

AI gadgets on mainstream bodies: watches, bands, glasses, earphones, and beyond. The technology makes it possible. The best step is likely ‘not so fast’.

And this is step one, not the last step. I wrote last year about [Apple exploring cameras across its device ecosystem](https://substack.com/redirect/253f3f5c-5f47-4db4-8eb4-f0f63f6784cb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA), AirPods included, and again this spring on [the clearer view of Apple’s AI wearables](https://substack.com/redirect/4c6693d2-9ec6-42af-9e6c-6b40c964a037?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).

Microphones on the wrist this year. My expectation is cameras in the ears next.

Compare Meta, which we covered Tuesday: Muse pointed at seven million pairs of glasses, going full speed, with a trust deficit. Apple has advantages here, because trust on privacy is the asset it protects, and [earning mainstream users’ trust is the next battle](https://substack.com/redirect/5eec1538-656e-4983-928d-272b5a604c33?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) in AI.

Apple has the trust, and Apple is the one saying not so fast. That is not an accident.

The technologies involved here are very interesting to a nerd like me, and it is very cool how they built this. Some of you may want to go into Apple’s report for the details.

### Q&A

Q1: Am I comfortable with the privacy tradeoffs of daily environmental monitoring on the Apple Watch?

ANSWER: on one of the features, yes. I love the Live Rewind idea: press the crown and get back the last fifteen seconds of whatever was said around you. Apple’s example was a waiter reading the specials, and a discreet rewind on the wrist to catch what you just missed would be kind of cool.

I have actually wished for that feature for a long time.

Especially useful with one’s spouse on occasion one attention wanders inadvertently.

At the same time, I am not sure I want the thing recording everything, all the time. And that is the core dilemma of our AI age ahead, not just from Apple but from all of these companies.

The rewards have to be clear and big, out of the box, and most ambient gadgets today do not meet that bar beyond early adopters.

Q2: What is the biggest issue for me in leaning into these AI services off local gadgets?

ANSWER: the trust factor, exactly as above. Trust is going to be earned slowly by all of these companies. They will all have to work hard at it, every day, over time, not put out proclamations that say we figured this out, trust us, it will be safe.

Especially given that billions of users now have the benefit of hindsight on how social media impacted our lives, not just for the better but for the worse, in terms of our children, our attention spans, our ability to absorb things and to do the harder work of remembering.

These technologies are extraordinary, but all of this has to move a little bit slower. It may take longer than the companies think. Even Apple. Not so fast.

## Clips from today

### AI Trust Is Earned, Not Proclaimed

The biggest issue in leaning into AI on the gadgets we wear is not the technology. It is whether the companies earn the trust, every day, instead of announcing it.

MP Take: Trust is going to be earned slowly by all of these companies. They will all have to work hard at it, every day, over time. Not put out proclamations that say we figured this out, trust us, it will be safe.

Billions of users now have the benefit of hindsight on how social media impacted our lives, not just for the better but for the worse. Our children, our attention spans, our ability to absorb things and do the harder work of remembering.

These technologies are extraordinary, but all of this has to move a little bit slower. Not so fast applies to every one of them.

### Apple Watch’s 15-Second Rewind Dilemma

Live Rewind gives you back the last fifteen seconds of whatever was said around you. Useful in the moment. Unsettling as a way of life.

MP Take: On Live Rewind, yes. Apple’s example was a waiter reading the specials, and a discreet rewind on the wrist to catch what you just missed would be kind of cool. I have wished for that feature for a long time.

At the same time, I am not sure I want the thing recording everything, all the time. And that is the core dilemma of our AI age ahead. Not just from Apple, but from all of these companies.

### AI’s Sugar High Has to Diffuse

Get into a Waymo and the thing drives itself. Extraordinary. And still a sugar high that has to work its way through an economy with slow speed bumps.

MP Take: The higher-probability scenario is more conservative than the current early enthusiasm, because we are in what I call the sugar high stage of AI. Oh my God, it can do this. And it can.

But it is a sugar high, a honeymoon phase. All of that has to diffuse into the economy, which has huge, slow speed bumps on the things that make it real. Moore’s Law tends to diffuse the productivity gains people are talking about; a lot gets displaced by the technology, and that comes out of current GDP numbers.

When you do the math it is super multi-variate and super complex. Even our best AI computers cannot project it with the precision some of these calculators imply. So be careful.

### OpenAI Cuts Price, Usage Up 10x

OpenAI’s CFO says a price cut on Luna sent usage up ten times and put OpenAI number one on OpenRouter, weeks before Anthropic files its IPO.

MP Take: There is a tactical and strategic neck-and-neck race between OpenAI and Anthropic. OpenAI was ahead. Anthropic caught up with a laser focus on the enterprise and on AI coding, a white-hot market, and is running ahead on revenue, over $65 billion annualized as of mid-July.

Now here is the CFO of the number two company saying we are testing elasticity in our markets and it is working. Anthropic has already blinked with cheaper pricing on its new Fable 5.1 model. OpenAI answered the same day with Astra.

This is now a daily, fluid metrics war, and it will run for quarters after both companies go public.
