ARD #151](https://substack.com/app-link/post?publication_id=684161&post_id=213131806&utm_source=post-email-title&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=8ne7oq&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo1MjMwMjM3MjIsInBvc3RfaWQiOjIxMzEzMTgwNiwiaWF0IjoxNzg3OTM0OTMwLCJleHAiOjE3OTA1MjY5MzAsImlzcyI6InB1Yi02ODQxNjEiLCJzdWIiOiJwb3N0LXJlYWN0aW9uIn0.eu2EUXRvQyVonHrDOvik0mHj83B1p4z9g0HLUPufijw)

### ...New DC tech tariffs weighed, a ‘dire’ AI cyber warning, & a judge blocks the Pentagon’s Anthropic blacklist.

‘Fumbling the Ball’ in US AI. All week, the bloom kept coming from the companies on this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/edda7298-d814-43c3-89d4-bc5ebd6f9e4c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA). Nvidia’s record quarter print. Its $13 billion Hugging Face M&Q deal. Apple’s September 9 iPhone launch.

Today is about the other side. The unforced errors. The US says it is in a race with China on AI. And it keeps fumbling its own ball. Three stories today, all self-inflicted: a tariff plan that would tax the AI buildout’s own inputs, a dire industry cyber warning, and a judge throwing out the Pentagon’s blacklist of Anthropic. Plus a $399 robot duck. My takes below, as discussed on the show.

## (1) Tariffing Your Own AI Buildout

First a report from Politico. The White House is weighing a new round of sweeping semiconductor tariffs, per eight people familiar. One version would go past chips. It would hit the products made with them: laptops, consoles, and the servers that fill AI data centers. Commerce Secretary Lutnick reportedly favors tying tariff relief to investment in US chip manufacturing. A phase-in is under discussion. The framework could still change.

Here is the problem. The industry is already short of high-end chips. Taiwan alone makes over 90 percent of the most advanced ones. New fabs cost billions and take years, if not decades. Until then, the buildout runs on imports. The industry’s own comparison for this buildout, per Politico: the transcontinental railroad. Tariff the rails, and the railroad slows.

My take: we wrote the two data-center primers this very week. Political football on Thursday, megawatts to gigawatts on Friday.That along with this month’s four-part AI supply-and-demand series said it plainly: compute is in short supply for the next five years.

Nvidia sits on a multi-trillion dollar backlog as the industry’s ‘Kingmaker’, now its uber investor and banker. I have long called TSMC the federal reserve of the global tech industry. That is the chain these tariffs would tax, along with the rest of the US tech company supply ecosystem from around the world.

So follow the implied logic. You say you are racing China. Then you consider taxing the exact inputs your racers run on. That is not defense. That is a fumble at your own goal line. The stated goal, onshoring chips to the US. Fine as a political goal with upcoming elections. The instrument taxes the AI wave’s own buildout while the fabs get built at prices significantly over real global market options. The political counter consists of phase-in and the investment-credit structure ‘sweeteners’ to soften it. The industry and individual companies of course will be compelled to vigorously lobby for exactly that.

And note the bigger reversal underneath. The globalized tech supply chain gave the US and the world better tech at lower prices, driven by Moore’s Law, for decades. Disinflation by design. Tariffs on the chain run that trend the other way. More on that in the overall take.

## (2) The Industry’s ‘Dire’ Cyber Warning

Second story, from Axios. OpenAI, Anthropic, Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, and more than 100 other companies signed an open letter Thursday. The message is stark. Organizations have months, not years, to prepare for AI-enabled cyberattacks. In the letter’s words: “We have a limited window to strengthen cyber defenses.” The targets they worry about are hospitals. Water systems. Power plants. Critical infrastructure.

The context makes it concrete. A recent attack on US water systems reportedly used an AI-generated exploitation script. AI is collapsing the time attackers need. The letter asks companies to raise their security bar, including on AI-generated code. It asks the frontier labs to lend defenders their best response models during major incidents. One caveat, per Axios: the letter carries no commitments, deadlines, or dollars. Yet.

My take: the warning is real, and we have been on this thread all summer. Our ‘Frontier Models Slip Their Cribs’ piece covered OpenAI’s escaped agents breaching Hugging Face itself. This week’s OpenAI technical report admitted the warning signs were missed for weeks.

Now note the fumble. While the labs were organizing collective defense, the government’s big contribution this summer was the ‘Blip 2.0’ pauses. Weeks of US-ordered halts aimed at its own frontier labs, in the name of safety. The attackers did not pause. If the window is truly months, the play is defense funding and coordination. Not benching your own players.

One more thing. Cyber defense is a global team sport. A balkanized tech world fights it worse, not better. Especially when the US government is also weighing separating open source/weight models from proprietary closed AI models. Another dropped ball, with the clock running.

## (3) The Pentagon’s Anthropic Blacklist, Blocked

Third story, from Reuters. A federal judge on Thursday blocked the Pentagon’s blacklisting of Anthropic. The background: Defense Secretary Hegseth had designated Anthropic a national-security supply-chain risk. It was the first time a US company was publicly tagged under that obscure procurement statute. The trigger, per Reuters: Anthropic’s refusal to allow Claude to be used for US surveillance or autonomous weapons. Anthropic says the label could cost it billions, weeks before its mega-AI IPO.

Judge Rita Lin’s 59-page order called the designation “illegal and baseless.” Her sharpest line: the invocation of national security is “not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics.” Anthropic’s suit argued First Amendment retaliation and a denial of due process. The company says it remains focused on working productively with the government. The Pentagon’s position: private companies should not constrain military action.

My take: put aside where you land on AI in weapons. That debate is real. We covered it back in ‘Shall We Play a Game’ in 2024. And credit to Dario Amodei and Anthropic for standing up. The fumble here is the politics behind the mechanics. The government blacklisted the leading US frontier lab, as it prepares the biggest AI IPO yet. A court threw it out as retaliation in under six months.

We have seen this dynamic before from the other Party as well. I called the Biden diffusion rule an ‘Unforced Error’ in January 2025. Different administration, same pattern. Aim at a rival, hit your own team. China does not need to do a thing. And note who caught the ball this time: the courts. The judicial referees are saving the US from its own fumbles.

My overall take: step back. Three stories, one pattern. Tariffs that would tax your own AI buildout. A cyber window closing while the government’s instinct was pausing its own labs. A blacklist of your leading frontier lab, thrown out as retaliation by your own courts. Nobody did this to the US. These are unforced errors. Fumbles, in a game the US keeps calling a race with China.

Now the highest-level frame. These moves pause, and in places reverse, the multi-decade globalization benefits that built US tech, from Hewlett-Packard in a garage to Silicon Valley. Remember what that system delivered. Apple got its iPhones made in China from 2007 through this year, hundreds of millions of them. At the peak, that supply chain employed over 33 million Chinese workers, talent and scale the US simply did not have. And the globalized chain gave everyone the great disinflationary trade: better tech at lower prices, Moore’s Law style, wave after wave. The US is now unilaterally opting out. Friend-shoring. Reshoring. Tariffs. Bans. All at the exact geopolitical moment it can least afford the fumbles. China is not waiting. It is building its markets across the Global South, without the US.

Here is what makes this wave different. Mainframes, minicomputers, PCs, the internet, mobile, cloud. None of those waves faced a balkanized world for US tech trade. This one does.

And there is a capital layer. Trillions are being raised for this AI buildout, for the chips and the power behind unimaginable amounts of tokens. The veteran economist Lacy Hunt, five decades in the bond markets, argued this week that the US has entered a new inflationary era, one that will prove bad for bonds. The interview is linked in the sources above. Treasury Secretary Bessent is working to stem the unwind of the decades-old, multi-trillionn dollar, global ‘[Japan yen trade](https://substack.com/redirect/c0f66eea-cc4f-47ca-b9a6-bced3e2dca4f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA).’ And US big tech has gone from ample free cash flow to almost none, with trillions of debt being added. We wrote that arc in the capex series. The disinflation machine is shifting into reverse, just as the biggest bills come due.

Meanwhile the American companies keep blooming through it. That was this whole week’s story on this [AI Tech Wave](https://substack.com/redirect/edda7298-d814-43c3-89d4-bc5ebd6f9e4c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA). Nvidia’s record print. The Hugging Face deal. Apple’s September setup. The bloom is real.

So are the dropped balls. The gap between them is policy, not capability. The US does not need to beat China so much as it needs to stop tackling itself.

## Gadget AI: Hugging Face’s Microduck

The Gadget AI today is a fun one. Hugging Face’s Microduck. A $399 micro-robot for developers, from its Pollen Robotics team. The specs, per Axios and the Pollen site: a 25-centimeter, open-source biped duck. No arms. An articulated beak that doubles as a gripper. Camera, mic, speaker, WiFi, Bluetooth, even a small lidar. It waddles. It follows a laser pointer. In the demo videos it picks up a dirty sock, rollerblades, and recovers from shoves. Preorders opened Thursday. It ships before Christmas. The tagline: ‘Made to move, ready to learn.’

The real pitch is the audience. This is for AI developers, as the first affordable robot you train yourself with reinforcement learning. Trial and error, on your desk. Hugging Face’s CEO says it was designed to fall and get up, so people learn that robots fail.

And remember whose duck this now is. Nvidia agreed to buy Hugging Face this week for $12.9 billion. That mirrors Stripe’s roughly $8 billion OpenRouter acquisition this month. Same logic, different layer. Nvidia is advancing its own, and global, open-source AI ambitions, the ‘Avengers’ alliance we have written about all summer. Millions of developers live on Hugging Face. The same millions utterly enamored with AI coding tools. Claude. Codex. And beyond. A cute $399 duck is a very smart way to greet them.

My take: enjoy the symmetry first. The same Hugging Face that OpenAI’s escaped agents breached last month is back in the news selling an adorable duck. From the center of the cyber story to the toy aisle in a week. I am getting one, by the way.

I was one of the first buyers of Sony’s Aibo robot dogs in the late 1990s, and I have owned almost every version since.

Now the serious frame. Investors are piling into AI robots. Elon’s Optimus. China’s recent robot conference, with sprinting robots reportedly clocking speeds past human record holders. Talking Usain Bolt speeds.

China robot leader Unitree’s IPO pop of several hundred percent recently highlights general enthusiasm in this category.

The lesson from two-plus decades of robot pets is consistent. They are all wonderful through the novelty phase. Then the practical question arrives: what does it DO? Until that answer is prosaic and daily, they stay toys. Delightful toys. The Microduck knows exactly what it is. Much of the robot investment wave has not answered that question yet.

And the irony ties the Gadget to all three of today’s stories. The physical world is a multi-decade AI opportunity. World models. Synthetic data production. Robots that learn by doing. We have written about each.

Winning it takes a vast manufacturing ecosystem built over decades of financial and human capital.

China has one. Ours is nascent. And the US moves are tariffs on the supply chain, reshoring mandates, and recent bans on Chinese robots and drones. On robots, the honest score is still: novelty leading, utility fumbling behind. Policy is fumbling right alongside.

### Q&A

Q1: What is MP’s earliest, favorite micro-robot?

ANSWER: Sony’s Aibo robot dog, from the late 1990s onward. A marvel of its time. Sony kept reviving it for over two decades, and I have followed the category ever since. I love pets, but not the taking-out and taking-care-of parts. The Aibo has been so much fun over the decades, version after version. The Verge’s CES piece on the 2018 revival captures the charm exactly.

Q2: What are the biggest lessons from the robotic toys?

ANSWER: They are extraordinary at connecting with us. [Anthropomorphized](https://substack.com/redirect/97dd5df1-f748-4d35-b80a-47bde7b4c5bf?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5lN29xIn0.bqmJ9TjykdtTXCviJ3jD2X5vxhprRGd5tpCaC89FQIA) as they are, the human instinct to bond with them emotionally is real. But they are all still in the novelty phase. Two-plus decades after Aibo, the honest answer to ‘what does it do for you daily’ is: still at the toy stage. That is a good reminder for the current AI robot investment wave. Watch for utility, not backflips. And it is why playing with the toys now is worthwhile. You are ready for these things when they are not toys. Otherwise we are fumbling this ball too, just more adorably.
