### The people who take the time to read with care are paying for the people who don’t. Here’s the skill I built to keep your own work out of that pile — and why no skill can do the part that matters.

If you didn’t read it, don’t send it to me.

Actually, I want to go a step farther than that.

If you don’t mean it, don’t send it to me.

You’ve paid this tax already. Sometimes in money. More often in the twenty minutes you spent last week working out if a document meant anything, and the sinking feeling that you were the first person who had actually read it.

I think a lot of us now open the internet with a reasonable level of suspicion that wasn’t there before. An email arrives and you wonder if the person who sent it knows what’s in it. A report lands and you wonder if anybody bothered to check the numbers. Somebody at work sends you a beautiful, six-page strategy document, but you have no idea if anyone has taken the time to read any of it.

We’ve traded the blank page for something that may be worse: acres and acres and acres of finished-looking pages that nobody has read, traveling at machine speed toward a person who is still expected to pay attention.

If the document reaches a customer, an executive meeting, a hiring decision, a forecast, or a pricing decision before anyone does that work, the cost stops being annoying and starts becoming real money and time you’re not getting back.

The sender got the speed. You got the bill.

There’s a way to keep the speed without accepting that bargain. It begins by treating authorship as a process again and putting AI inside that process as a tool. I’ve built a pro-authorship skill to do the part a universal checklist can’t. It finds the choices that make the work yours, then teaches the model to preserve them instead of handing you somebody else’s idea of good writing.

Here’s what’s inside:

- What slop actually costs. A government department, an open-source maintainer, and a federal judge — with the invoices and the hours.

- Why technique doesn’t save you. 758 consultants in a controlled trial, and the group briefed on prompt engineering did worst of all.

- Why AI didn’t reduce the number of drafts. It made each pass faster. I still do dozens, and the wrestling is where I find out what I actually think.

- What it means for your career. Human attention is the scarce thing now. Being worth reading is the advantage.

- The pro-authorship skill. Give it a paragraph you rejected and the version you sent, and it works out what you were protecting, then teaches your AI to keep it.
